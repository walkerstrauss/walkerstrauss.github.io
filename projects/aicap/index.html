Voice and touch for wine recommendations

I led interaction design for Onki’s in-store wine kiosk: the research, the spec the engineers built from, and the assistant’s prompts and evals.

Problem

A wine aisle can hold hundreds of bottles and few people who can talk about them.

Staff are busy or on break, and shoppers who don’t know much about wine often don’t ask. In our survey, the most common complaints about buying wine in a store were too many options (9 of 19) and not enough guidance (8 of 19).

Solution

A screen at eye level that a shopper can speak to, type on, or tap.

The kiosk is a screen at eye level in the wine aisle. A shopper can speak, type, or tap. It recommends a few bottles, explains how each one fits what they asked for, and says where to find it on the shelf.

I wrote the interaction specification the engineers built from: second-by-second rules and acceptance criteria for how voice and touch work together. The decisions below come from it.

Competitor research

We studied an earlier wine kiosk, wine apps, and other self-serve screens people use in public.

User research

Shoppers wanted to set the pace, and only one wanted voice alone.

We surveyed 35 shoppers about how they buy wine, and I clustered the answers to find the kinds of shoppers we were designing for. Twenty answered the questions about the kiosk itself. I also talked with the sommelier at the store where the kiosk would go about how they approach shoppers, so the flow could follow that conversation.

Literature review

I also reviewed research on voice assistants in public, how people respond to recommendations, and how they shop in stores. Each decision below opens with what it took from that.

Design

Five stages, from a shopper browsing the aisle to a recommendation.

At each stage, what the kiosk asks of the shopper and what it shows change.

Decision 1 · First contact

The kiosk speaks first.

Saying a phrase out loud to a machine in an aisle is awkward, so a shopper never has to. The kiosk stays quiet while someone browses, offers a way in as they come nearer, and greets them itself at arm’s length. “Let’s start” opens the same session by touch.

Decision 2 · Gathering

Every step works by speech, text or tap.

Spoken input appears as text while the shopper talks, and each preference becomes a chip they can check and remove. “Show me matches” switches on once there is something to match; “Just surprise” is always available for a shopper who wants to skip ahead.

Decision 3 · Recommendation

The card follows the voice.

The voice gives the short pitch: name, price, origin, a taste, and where to find the bottle. The card holds the detail to scan. To keep the two together, I had the assistant say a short detail sentence just before it names the first wine, so the card turns as that sentence starts.

Decision 4 · Recommendation

A tap stops the voice.

Shoppers tap while the assistant is still talking. In the spec, a tap is an interruption: the voice stops, the caption finishes its phrase so nothing is cut off mid-word, and the assistant answers the tap instead of finishing its old plan.

Decision 5 · Back to gathering

The assistant recommends early, then refines.

Early versions questioned shoppers until it felt like an interrogation. The assistant now never asks more than one question in a row without recommending a wine. Each answer narrows the next set, and earlier picks stay in the carousel as history.

Testing

My prompts graded consistently in the B range; the originals, around C.

I built the evals: five simulated shoppers (an excited newcomer, an anxious one, an insecure one, a controlling one and one looking for validation), each conversation graded on a rubric and scored as a mean and spread per shopper. Each prompt change answered something a test or a tester found:

  • Repeated phrases and full wine names said again: the assistant now refers back to a wine the way a person would.
  • The price read out every time, though it was on the card: now only when the shopper asks.
  • Too many questions: once the shopper gives a concrete cue, it moves on.
  • A tester said they were under the drinking age and still got suggestions: I proposed a top tier of rules above everything else, starting with no invented wines and nothing off-topic.
Outcome

The kiosk built from the spec made a sale in its first store pilot.

After my part ended, Onki’s first kiosk pilot, at a wine and spirits store in Tribeca in February 2026, produced a confirmed sale. A three-store New York pilot began in August 2026. Onki’s milestones
