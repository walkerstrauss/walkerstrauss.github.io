Mah’s Jong

A two-player race to build a mahjong hand, made by a team of nine students in C++ on the CUGL framework. As project lead I set each sprint’s tasks and priorities with the software lead, and I built several of the game’s scenes and systems.

The game

Two players race to complete a mahjong hand.

A full hand is four sets of three and a pair. Each turn a player draws from the pile or the discard, then discards. Taking the discard means revealing a permanent set. The pile is a visible layered grid, and eight zodiac “Celestial” tiles help a player or disrupt their opponent.

Leading the team

Every task in a sprint had an owner, a due date and a priority.

The team was nine programmers and designers, with a software lead, a design lead and a UX lead. The software lead and I decided together what each sprint would deliver and in what order. I set every task’s owner, due date, expected time and priority from 1 to 5, and wrote the detailed task descriptions the team worked from.

I also ran the handoff between the designers and the programmers. Designers built each screen in Figma and exported it; programmers loaded it into the game. In that sprint, loading every new scene was my own top-priority task.

Decision 1

The team turned a scoring game against a boss into a one-on-one race.

The first rules had draw, play and score phases, a shop, and a “Grandma” boss with hit points. The team’s revised design dropped the scoring and the boss. I removed the grandma tiles and the score display from the build.

Decision 2

Special tiles became Celestial tiles, with three roles.

The designers grouped the eight tiles as Support, Risky and Disruptor: help yourself, gamble, or get in the opponent’s way. I took part in brainstorming the early action-tile designs and built the animations that show an opponent’s Celestial tile taking effect.

Decision 3

The game ends each turn itself and shows what the player can do next.

I first built an end-turn button, then limited it to the moments a turn could end, then replaced it with automatic turn ending and on-screen guides for each action.

I also built the turn timer, which ends a turn when it runs out, and lengthened it from 30 to 45 seconds. A teammate later reworked the timers.

Decision 4

The tutorial became a single-player scene you play through.

A teammate built the first tutorial. I rebuilt it with another programmer as a scene the player plays through, from a designer’s Figma layout.

My role

Project lead, and a programmer on the game’s scenes.

As project lead I set the team’s tasks and priorities with the software lead, wrote the detailed task descriptions, and ran the handoff between the designers and the programmers. In code I built the discard pile and its interface; the settings, game-over, info and help scenes; and the AnimatedNode class. I started the animation controller and the tutorial scene, which teammates and I finished together.
