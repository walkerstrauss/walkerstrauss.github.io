Savoring Weather Clock

An interactive, AI-powered home appliance that turns live weather into a prompt to notice it.

Built with Arduino UNO R4 WiFi, Google Maps Weather API, OpenAI API

Problem

People check the weather on a phone and move on.

The lab was exploring technology that helps people slow down. The question for this project was how an everyday object could make the weather something a person notices.

Solution

A clock for the home that shows the weather with light, a turning vane and a line of text.

The clock sits on a wall or a shelf at home and reads the live weather. When the weather changes, its window plays a pattern for it, the vane turns, and a language model writes a line from the live weather for the display. A touch asks for a new line.

Research

The design draws on savoring: the techniques people use to notice a good moment and make it last.

Three things I read shaped what the clock does.

Decision 1 · Interaction design

The clock moves only when the weather changes.

I listed three ways the clock could decide when to speak: when a part of the device moves, on a timer, or when the person asks. The clock writes a new line on every check and on a touch, and keeps light and motion for the moment the weather itself changes.

Decision 2 · Conversation design

Each line carries a savoring technique without naming it.

The words are written by a language model, so the design is in the prompt. I tested it until its lines carried the technique without naming or teaching it.

Decision 3 · Conversation design

Each line appreciates the weather first, then suggests one small act.

Gratitude has a larger effect in the moment, so each line opens with appreciation. The act that follows comes from one of four kinds of sustainable behavior in the prompt (ecological, frugal, altruistic or equitable), and the model is told not to mention sustainability.

Decision 4 · Interaction design

A motor turns the vane when the weather changes.

The vane is the clock’s playful piece: when a motor turns it, the movement is enough to get a person’s attention without forcing them to engage.

Decision 5 · Interaction design

The light plays the weather.

I considered three triggers for the light: an idle animation, a signal while a line is being written, or a response when someone comes near or touches the clock. The built clock ties the light to the weather.

System

One loop keeps the light, the motor, the display and the touch in step.

I wrote the code as separate modules with one loop over them. We first planned an ESP32 with indoor air sensors; the clock as built is an Arduino UNO R4 WiFi working from live weather alone.

Six fields, seven kinds of weather
The board asks the Google Maps Weather API for current conditions and keeps six fields. The condition type, one of 44, becomes one of seven categories.
A turn that never blocks the loop
The motor is timed against the board’s own counter, so a touch still registers while the vane turns.
A reply is checked before it is shown
The reply is read key by key. If anything is missing, the display says so and the reason goes to the serial log.
Process and roles

November to February, from first ideas to the clock.

Each week Gaeul and I sketched concepts and wrote short descriptions of possible interactions, then refined them with Professor Yoon. Once the direction was set, Xinyi Zhou joined to prototype the motor behavior and lighting.

Outcome

Built, shown, and now being piloted in people’s homes.

The pilot is still running, and a paper is in preparation (Han, Strauss, Zhou & Yoon).
